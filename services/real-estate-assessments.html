Why this matters in a rural transaction

Most rural BC properties run on septic, not municipal sewer — and unlike a furnace or a roof, a septic system is mostly invisible until it isn't. Lenders increasingly want documentation before funding, buyers want to know what they're inheriting, and sellers want a number before a buyer's inspector finds a problem for them. All of that has to happen inside a subject removal window that's usually measured in days, not weeks.

What's included in an assessment

  • Site visit — locating and inspecting the tank(s), field, and any pumps or control equipment
  • Assessment of visible condition: baffles, tank integrity, signs of field failure or surfacing effluent
  • Where relevant, a look at available records — permits, as-builts, or health authority filings on the property
  • A written report: current condition, a repair-vs-replace recommendation, an estimated remaining service life, and a cost range
  • Coordination with a pump-out if the tank needs to be emptied for a full inspection

Who orders one, and when

We get calls from buyers' agents, sellers getting ahead of an issue before it becomes a negotiating point, and occasionally lenders directly. The most common trigger is a home inspector flagging the septic system as outside their scope and recommending a specialist — which is standard, since most general inspectors don't assess septic systems in detail.
